# The Derren Brown System

The memory section of *Tricks of the Mind* (Derren Brown, 2006) is a complete practical course.
This file follows its order: linking, loci and memory palaces, the peg system, the number code,
long numbers, a deck of cards, football scores, names, and reviewing. Brown's techniques are
paraphrased here with his worked examples, plus guidance on teaching them to anyone. His point:
none of this is a gift. It's technique plus practice, presented as a gift for the show.

Read [advanced-systems.md](advanced-systems.md) for the full tables built on these methods
(00–99 images, all 52 cards).

---

## 1. The linking system

**Just link each thing to the next thing** with a visual image. Three rules make the image stick:

1. **Vivid and emotionally engaging**: you should *feel* something (laugh, wince, be disgusted).
2. **The elements interact**: they do something to each other, rather than sitting side by side.
3. **Unusual, not normal**: an ordinary scene is a forgotten scene.

**The classic first exercise** (Brown's practice list). Link these 20 in order, then recite them
forwards and backwards:

> telephone → sausage → monkey → button → book → cabbage → glass → mouse → stomach →
> cardboard → ferry → Christmas → athlete → key → wigwam → baby → kiwi → bed → paintbrush → walnut

E.g. a telephone that rings, you pick it up and it's a greasy *sausage* that wriggles out of your
hand and is snatched by a *monkey*, who is struggling to do up a huge *button* on its waistcoat…

Teaching tip: let the learner make *their own* scene for each pair. Ask "what does the sausage
do to the monkey?" Self-made images stick far better than supplied ones.

**Drawback:** if one connection is lost, the rest of the chain is lost with it. That's why loci
come next.

## 2. The loci system and memory palaces

**Loci**: attach each image to a place along a familiar real-life route you know well.
Recall = walk the route. A forgotten item leaves a gap but doesn't break the rest.

**Brown's rules for building palaces:**
- **Expand the loci in each room.** A kitchen isn't one locus; it's the fridge, the cutlery
  drawer, the oven… Keep rooms uncluttered so images stay clear and distinct.
- **Put the action *inside* things** ("Dave sat *in* the fridge"), so something else can happen
  *next to* the fridge without the two getting in each other's way.
- **Distinction matters more than distance.** Classical advice put loci ~30 feet apart; Brown
  finds that unnecessary. What matters is that loci don't resemble each other.
- **Fixed order, always.** Brown moves **clockwise, starting with whatever is on his immediate
  left**, and works around the room.
- **Markers every five loci** (a Greek idea): a hand with fingers splayed at the 5th locus, a
  marker at the 10th, and so on. To recall item 12, go straight to the "ten" marker and step
  two more. Brown also uses special markers for meaningful boundaries (a toy spinning at the
  top of the stairs to mark "the turn of the century").
- **Permanent information gets its own buildings.** Day-to-day loci (tasks, speeches) can be
  allowed to fade and reused, since the loci stay fixed and the objects fade. Valuable
  information goes into other designated rooms, spaces or buildings that you keep.
- **Grow a sprawling palace.** Add an imaginary extra door to your house that opens into another
  familiar place. Brown "takes" museum floors, London parks, parts of his school and university,
  theatres he performed in, stately homes, and friends' homes, and links them with grand
  stairwells and marble hallways (inspired by Hannibal Lecter's palace in Thomas Harris's
  *Hannibal*).
- **Choose places that are vivid and emotionally neutral.** Places that are dim in memory, or
  mixed with good and bad associations, distance you from the picture. You should be able to
  look out of your own eyes inside it.
- **Prefer real places to invented ones.** Inventing a place means memorising *it* first.
  When you find a suitable real place, memorise it deliberately: walk a fixed route, photograph
  it, return a few times, ideally when it isn't crowded. The loci must be effortless, as
  easy as the rooms in your own home.
- **Re-awaken palaces from time to time** by wandering through them in quiet moments, so items
  don't fade. Brown notes the trip becomes pleasant and almost auto-hypnotic.

**Brown's showpiece:** he memorised all 39 Shakespeare plays in order of writing in about ten
minutes, using the interior of a theatre he was performing in. It works because he:
- *mixes* fixed images with **moving characters who interact with him**: two grand gentlemen
  offer him a mug (*Two Gentlemen of Verona*), and a schoolboy keeps stepping the same way as him
  on the stairs (*Comedy of Errors*);
- uses **number images** for counts and sequels: "mug" = 39 in his code (so 39 plays), and Prince
  Harry with *bees* (6) = *Henry VI*, with a *whore* (4) = *Henry IV*, with a *hive* (5) = *Henry V*;
- uses **sound-alikes** for titles: a periscope = *Pericles*, coral = *Coriolanus*, cymbals =
  *Cymbeline*, a pig and ham = *Hamlet*;
- adds a **marker** for the turn of 1600.

Teaching tip: after a learner has built one 10-locus room, have them try a "Brown journey":
10 items, with at least 3 placed as moving characters.

## 3. The peg system

Linking and loci handle lists. **Pegs handle digits**: reference numbers, hotel rooms, phone
numbers, PINs, and "what is item 7?". Numbers aren't words and can't be pictured, so the trick
is to turn each number into a word.

**Rhyme pegs (1–10)**, the simple version:

| 1 bun | 2 shoe | 3 tree | 4 door | 5 hive | 6 sticks | 7 heaven | 8 gate | 9 line | 10 hen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Link item *n* to peg *n* (e.g. telephone at position 1 → an iced *bun* used as a phone, with
currants for a keypad).

Rhyming breaks down in the teens. One workaround repeats the same pegs per group of ten with a
modifier (11–20 on fire, 21–30 freezing, 31–40 bright blue). Brown isn't a fan of it and
prefers the **number code** below. That's what we teach as the main system.

## 4. Brown's number code (digits → consonants)

Each digit becomes a consonant, chosen for whichever letter connects most easily with that number:

| Digit | Letter(s) | Why (Brown's reasons) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | z / s | z is in "zero"; s sounds similar |
| 1 | l | they look similar |
| 2 | n | two downward strokes on a small n |
| 3 | m | three downward strokes |
| 4 | r | fou**r** |
| 5 | f / v | **f**i**v**e: similar sounds |
| 6 | b / p | b looks like 6; p and b sound and look similar |
| 7 | t | 7 and capital T look alike |
| 8 | ch / sh / j | the "gh" in "eight"; j is the nearest sound |
| 9 | g | a written g resembles 9 |

**Fill in only vowels, h and y** (w too, by extension) when making words. Never add a letter
that's already in the code: "notch" contains t (7), so it decodes as 278, not 28. Use
`memgym.py decode <word>` to check a word.

**Basic images for 0–9:** zoo, ale, hen, ham, whore, hive, bee, tea, shoe, goo. Brown's pegs
are deliberately crude where it helps ("whore" for 4), because crude images stick.

This is **the only number code this skill uses**: every number, card and date technique is built
on it. Don't mix in other digit-to-letter systems.

**PIN example (Brown's):** 1743 → L T R M → "**Light Room**" or "**Le Tram**". Link that to the
idea of the cash machine: a cash machine in a dazzling white room full of lights, or stepping
off a tram in France to grab euros. At the ATM, the cash machine triggers the image and you
decode it.

## 5. Remembering long numbers

Use **meaning wherever the digits offer it, and code words where they don't**, woven into one
story. Brown's worked example for `8764 9847 4505 7734 9872 4`:

- **8764**: a countdown from 8 (8-7-6-…) where the counter misses 5 and stops: **8764**.
- **98**: fair enough, she's nearly a hundred: **98** years old.
- **474**: she even gets aeroplanes muddled up: a **474** instead of a 747.
- **505**: the aeroplanes fly over her beehives (5 = hive). She had three hives in a row but the
  middle one is missing: **5-0-5**.
- **773**: so she put a **ToTeM** pole (T-T-M = 773) in its place.
- **498**: she did this for her **98**th birthday (the 4 joins it from the chain).
- **724**: I only gave her a **TeNneR** (T-N-R = 724) as a gift.

The **links** between chunks are what make it work. Each chunk hands you the next. For very long
numbers, place each chunk's image along a palace instead of one story. For drilling, use the fixed
00–99 images (advanced-systems.md) so you're not inventing words under time pressure.

## 6. Memorising a deck of cards

Each card becomes a word from its **suit initial + rank code**: **S**pades, **H**earts,
**D**iamonds, **C**lubs, plus the rank's consonant.
- 9 of Hearts → H + 9 → H-g → "**Hag**"
- 6 of Spades → S + 6 → S-b → "**Sob**"

Place the cards along a palace in order, linking each card's image to its locus: if the 9♥ is at
position 1, a hag is drinking ale (1) at your first locus.

**Court cards are characters, not codes.** "J" would clash with j = 8, so give the Jacks,
Queens and Kings **themed people per suit**: Hearts = love and weddings (Cupid, bride, groom),
Spades = death and digging (a boy digging, a sinister housekeeper, the Devil), and so on.
The full 52-card starter set is in advanced-systems.md.

Practise: `memgym.py gen cards 13` → 26 → 52.

## 7. Memorising football scores (and any paired data)

The data's own letters become part of the words. Brown's example: Everton 2 – Watford 0 in the
1983–84 season:
- **84** → ch-r → **chair**.
- **E2 W0** → E, n, W, s → **E N W S** → the compass points: East North West South.
- Image: a **chair** floating and spinning to point East, North, West, South.
- 1984–85: Man United 1 – Everton 0 → **85** = sh-v → **shave**; **M1 E0** → M L E S →
  **Miles** → shaving while running miles.
- 1985–86: Liverpool 3 – Everton 1 → **86** = sh-p → **shop**; **L3 E1** → L M E L →
  **lamb + eels** → a shop selling lamb and eels.

Generalise: **entity initials + numbers → words**, for exam marks, sales figures, sports
results, KPIs by team, flight numbers by city, port numbers by service.

## 8. Remembering names

**Route 1: someone you already know.** Bring to mind a friend, relative or famous person with
the same name. Imagine the new person has been made up to look a bit like them, or is
impersonating them.

**Route 2: name → image → feature → link.**
1. **Turn the name into an image**: Mike → a **microphone**; Alice → an **Alice band**;
   Bill → an **invoice** (a bill).
2. **Find something memorable about their features**, size or appearance. Something around the
   face is best and quickest: a beard, glasses, a large nose, a scar or mark, spiky hair. Use
   clothing if something stands out. Maybe their face reminds you of someone, or of an animal.
3. **Link the two together**: Mike's large nose becomes a microphone stuck in the middle of his
   face. Bill puts on his small rimmed glasses to read the bill after dinner.

Then use the name in conversation and review it before you leave.

## 9. The importance of reviewing

Every technique above makes an image strong but young. Review makes it permanent:

| Review | When |
|---|---|
| 1 | Immediately: walk it once forwards and once backwards |
| 2 | ~1 hour later (or at the end of the session) |
| 3 | That evening or next morning |
| 4 | +3 days |
| 5 | +1 week |
| 6 | +3 weeks |
| 7 | +2 months, then ×2.5 each time |

A failed recall means the *image* was weak. Rebuild it (more vivid, more interaction, more
personal) and restart its schedule. Don't just re-read it.
