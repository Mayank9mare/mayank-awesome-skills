Memory Palace Workbook

Remember a list of twenty things in order, a PIN, a shuffled deck, the name of everyone at the table. None of it takes a gift. It takes about six techniques and some practice, and you can feel the first one working in two minutes.

Notes from Tricks of the Mind (Derren Brown, 2006), turned into a hands-on course.

    How memory works

    Every technique on this page runs the same four-step loop. You can't remember what you never noticed, and you can't picture a word or a number until you turn it into something you can see.

    Brown's starting point: memory is association. Pictures are far easier to remember than words and numbers, so we convert everything into pictures and lock each one onto something we already know. A picture sticks when it follows three rules:

    • VividMake it emotionally engaging. Laugh, wince, be disgusted. A picture you feel is a picture you keep.
    • InteractingThe two things must do something to each other. A monkey snatching a sausage, not a monkey next to a sausage.
    • UnusualOrdinary is forgettable. Make it huge, absurd, moving, wrong.
    Is a perfect memory possible?True hyperthymesia (highly superior autobiographical memory) is a rare, inborn condition, and a truly photographic memory hasn't held up in testing. What you can train is plenty: in a 2017 study, ordinary people who practised the memory palace for about 30 minutes a day for six weeks more than doubled their recall and kept most of the gain months later.

    The linking system

    Just link each thing to the next thing.

    Make one picture out of item 1 and item 2. Then one out of item 2 and item 3. Each picture holds only two things, and each one hands you the next.

    1. Picture the first item clearly. Tie it to the reason for the list (the shop, the speech, the trip).
    2. Ask "what does item 1 do to item 2?" and see it happen, big and strange, for two or three seconds.
    3. Move on: item 2 does something to item 3, and so on.
    4. Recall by starting at item 1 and letting each picture hand you the next. Then try it backwards.

    Use it for: shopping lists, the points of a talk without notes, steps of a procedure, a set of jokes.

    The catchIf one connection is lost, the whole rest of the chain is lost. For anything long or important, use a palace.
    4

    The loci system and memory palaces

    Instead of chaining items to each other, attach each picture to a place along a route you already know well. To recall, walk the route. A forgotten item leaves one gap; the rest stay put.

    Brown's rules for a good palace

    • Move clockwise, starting with whatever is on your immediate left. Always the same order.
    • Many loci per room. A kitchen isn't one locus: it's the fridge, the cutlery drawer, the oven.
    • Put the action inside things. Dave sits in the fridge, so something else can happen next to it without the two getting in each other's way.
    • Distinct beats distant. Loci don't need to be far apart, but they must not look alike.
    • A marker every five loci, such as a hand with fingers splayed at number 5. To find item 12, go straight to the "ten" marker and step two more.
    • Grow it into a palace. Imagine an extra door in your house that opens into another place you know: your school, a park, a theatre, a museum floor. Keep valuable information in buildings you never reuse.
    • Use real places you know vividly, without mixed feelings. When you find a good one, walk a fixed route, take a photo, and go back a few times.
    • Re-walk your palaces now and then so the pictures don't fade.
    Brown's showpieceHe memorised all 39 Shakespeare plays, in the order they were written, in about ten minutes, using the theatre he was performing in. Two grand gentlemen offer him a mug (39 in his number code, so 39 plays: Two Gentlemen of Verona). A schoolboy keeps stepping the same way as him on the stairs (Comedy of Errors). Prince Harry appears with bees (6) buzzing round his head (Henry VI). Characters move and interact with him, and that's what makes it stick.

    The peg system

    Linking and loci handle lists. Pegs handle numbers: "what's item 7?", a hotel room, a reference number. Numbers aren't pictures, so give each one a word you can picture. The simplest version rhymes:

    1bun
    2shoe
    3tree
    4door
    5hive
    6sticks
    7heaven
    8gate
    9line
    10hen

    To remember that item 1 is a telephone: an iced bun used as a phone, with currants for a keypad.

    Rhymes run out in the teens, and they can't store a phone number. Brown prefers a more powerful system, next.

    The number code

    Turn each digit into a consonant. Then add vowels to make a word, and the word becomes a picture. These are Brown's letters, each chosen because it's easy to connect with its number:

    A PIN in ten seconds

    1743 → L T R M → "Light Room" or "Le Tram". Link it to the cash machine. At the ATM, the machine brings back the room full of lights, and you decode it.

    Long numbers

    Brown's trick for long numbers: use any meaning the digits already have, and use code words where they have none, all woven into one silly story. Each part hands you the next. Tap or hover a line to see its digits.

    876498474505773498724
    1. 8764: I hear a countdown from 8, but the counter misses 5 and stops.
    2. 98: Fair enough, she's nearly a hundred: 98 years old.
    3. 474: She even gets aeroplanes muddled up, a 474 instead of a 747.
    4. 505: The planes fly over her beehives (5 = hive). Three in a row, but the middle one is missing.
    5. 773: So she put a ToTeM pole (t-t-m) in its place.
    6. 498: She did this for her 98th birthday.
    7. 724: I only gave her a TeNneR (t-n-r) as a gift.

    For very long numbers, put each chunk's picture at a locus in a palace instead of one story.

    Memorising a deck of cards

    Each card becomes a word: the suit's initial plus the rank's code letter. Then place the 52 pictures along a palace in order.

    Football scores, and any pairs

    The data's own letters become part of the words. Brown's example is Everton 2, Watford 0 in the 1983–84 season:

    The next season, Man United 1, Everton 0 in 1984–85: 85 is sh-v (shave), and M1 E0 gives M L E S (miles), so someone is shaving while running miles. The same method works for exam marks, sales figures, flight numbers, or port numbers for each service.

    Remembering names

    Most "bad with names" is never having really heard the name. Listen, repeat it, use it once. Then use one of Brown's two routes:

    Route 1: someone you already know

    Think of a friend, relative or famous person with the same name. Imagine the new person has been made up to look a bit like them, or is impersonating them.

    Route 2: name, feature, link

    1. Name → picture. Mike becomes a microphone, Alice an Alice band, Bill an invoice.
    2. Find a feature. Something around the face is quickest: a beard, glasses, a large nose, a scar, spiky hair. Or their clothes, or the animal their face reminds you of.
    3. Link the two. Mike's large nose becomes a microphone stuck in the middle of his face. Bill puts on his small rimmed glasses to read the bill after dinner.

    The importance of reviewing

    A new picture is strong but young. Each time you recall it, it lasts longer. Recall before you check: that act of pulling it out is what makes it stick.

    If you miss one, the picture was too weak. Don't re-read it: rebuild it bigger, stranger and more personal, then start its schedule again.

    Beyond the book

    Fiction gives us two more "perfect memories". One is a real technique; the other is a fantasy with a useful real version.

    Patrick Jane: notice everything

    The Mentalist's memory palace is built on the carnival he grew up in, and his real skill is attention. Practise Kim's game: look at 15 objects for a minute, cover them, list them. In a new room, scan in a fixed order (exits, people, hands, anything out of place), then close your eyes and describe it. After a meeting, walk back through the room you sat in: each object holds one point that was made there.

    Mike Ross: read it once

    Suits' instant eidetic memory is fiction, and speed-reading trades away understanding. What works: skim the contents first and give each section a locus, so you know where everything lives. Then place the key claim and its numbers at each locus. For exact wording, write the first letter of each word and recite from those letters, adding one line at a time.

    A calendar in your head

    People with real hyperthymesia can name the weekday of any date. You can calculate it with Conway's Doomsday method. Each century has an anchor (1900s Wednesday, 2000s Tuesday). The year shifts it by ⌊yy/12⌋ + yy mod 12 + ⌊(yy mod 12)/4⌋. And 4/4, 6/6, 8/8, 10/10, 12/12, 9/5, 5/9, 7/11, 11/7 and the last day of February always share that weekday.

    Six weeks, thirty minutes a day

    Week 1The twenty-word list by linking, then by loci. Build two palaces of 20 loci each (clockwise, a marker every five). Learn the number code and the pictures for 0–9.
    Week 2Two-digit pictures 10–99, checking each word in the converter. Drill both ways (number → picture, picture → number). Try a 20-digit number as a story.
    Week 3Learn the 52 card pictures, one suit a day. Memorise 13 cards, then 26. Learn the Doomsday method: five dates a day.
    Week 4Speed: a full deck, untimed then timed. Forty digits in under five minutes. Build two more palaces from places you visit.
    Week 5Real life: one real thing a day. A talk, a document section, a meeting, the names at a gathering.
    Week 6Mix the drills. Walk a palace you built four weeks ago without looking. Set your own targets.

    After that, ten minutes a day keeps it sharp. Compare yourself only with yourself last week.
